1. Divide the image height into three equal portions corresponding to each channel (`preprocess.py:split_channels`).
2. Further crop predefined margins off each channel image (`preprocess.py:crop_margin`).

# Multi-Scale Alignment: Gaussian Image Pyramid
## Implementation

The Gaussian Image Pyramid was implemented through a recursive algorithm:
1. Blur and downsample both target image and reference image by applying fourth binomial filter $b_4 = [1, 4, 6, 4, 1] / 16$ with a stride 2 ([source](https://visionbook.mit.edu/pyramids_new_notation.html#gaussian-pyramid)).
   - The fourth binomial is a close approximation to a Gaussian with $\sigma = 1$.
   - Details on how to efficiently apply a 1D kernel to a 2D image discussed in this [section](#fast-two-pass-kernel-operation).
2. Function is recursively called on these downsampled and blurred images, returning an optimal shift `D_down` from a lower level.
3. `D_down` is used as a starting point for searching in our current level. We shift the target image by `D_est = D_down * 2` (since images scale by a factor of 2 moving up the pyramid) before searching in the current level for optimal `D_best`.
   - In the lowest level of the pyramid (base case for our recursion), we search within `[-15, 15] px` (`window_coarse = 15`).
   - In all other levels, we search within `[-2, 2] px` (`window_fine = 2`).
   - We search a large window at the coarsest (lowest) level because downsampled image is small and searching is cheapest there.
   - Since every level doubles distance, a $\pm 15$ window at the lowest level covers a search of $±15 \cdot 2^(L−1)$ at full resolution, which should be able to cover plausible misalignments.
   - The general idea is to find a good starting estimate in the lowest level and then add small corrections going back up the pyramid.
   - As we shall see in the failure case, this also means that any error in the base case will double and accumulate up every level.
4. We return total accumulated shift `D_est + D_best` from deepest level to current level.

The stopping condition or base case for our recursive pyramid was chosen when `np.min(img.shape) < self.min_dim` (heuristically `min_dim = 64 px`). 

**Script(s):**  
`python main.py --method pyramid --metric euclidean`  
`python main.py --method pyramid --metric ncc`

### Fast Two-Pass Kernel Trick
The 2D [$B_4$ binomial kernel](https://stevengong.co/notes/Binomial-Filter) (and Gaussian kernel) are seperable. 

Recall that a 2D filter kernel is separable if it can be expressed as the outer product of two vectors ([source](https://blogs.mathworks.com/steve/2006/10/04/separable-convolution/)).

$$
B_4 = \frac{1}{256}
\begin{bmatrix}
1&4&6&4&1 \\
4&16&24&16&4\\
6&24&36&24&6\\
4&16&24&16&4\\
1&4&6&4&1
\end{bmatrix}
= \tfrac{1}{16}\begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 4\\ 6\\ 4\\ 1\end{bmatrix}\tfrac{1}{16}\begin{bmatrix}1 & 4 & 6 & 4 & 1\end{bmatrix} = b_4^\top b_4
$$

Therefore, applying a column blur followed by a row blur is equivalent to applying a 2D kernel.

Applying a $k \times k$ 2D kernel to a $n \times m$ images has time complexity $O(k^2(n + m)) = O(k^2n + k^2m)$.   
Applying a $k$ size kernel once vertically to $m$ column pixels and once horizontally to $n$ row pixels has time complexity $O(kn + km)$.

In `pyramid.py:GaussianPyramid:gaussian_blur_img`, we apply convolve the image with the $b_4$ first on columns (line 37) and then on rows (line 42). Here, rather than sliding the kernel one pixel at a time, we shift image by each kernel offset and add the five shifted copies, each weighed by its entry of $b_4$. 

Every shift replaces every pixel at a location with the pixel's neighbor at that offset. Convolving each of the five shifted copies by the corresponding kernel gives every pixel the same weighted neighbourhood sum as sliding the kernel over it - with the difference that now we can compute it for the whole image at once. This is known as tap-by-tap or shift-and-add convolution (as opposed to sliding convolution).

### Discussion on `00056v.jpg`
The visual artifacts in `00056v.jpg` result from damage (or stains) to the individual glass plates rather than resulting from alignment error.
We visualize these individual defects by:
1. Aligning the plates using `np.roll` and the shifts (red: [13, 1], green: [5, 1]).
2. Drop rows that were wrapped by the `np.roll` operation so they don't count as defects.
3. Standardize each plate so all pixels share common color space.
4. Calculate the per-pixel median of the three channels - median is unaffected by stain or defect on each plate.
5. Subtract the median from each plate to obtain the residual, which quantifies how much each plate disagrees with the other two.
6. Apply a high-pass Gaussian blur filter ($\sigma=16$ chosen visually) to remove broad tonal differences and keep sharp, smaller differences (characteristic of stains).

{% include figure.html src="/assets/a1/00056v_stains.png" caption="Per-plate residual against the per-pixel median of the three standardised, aligned plates, high-pass filtered (σ = 8 px).<br>Blue means that plate is darker than the other two there. Conversely, red means brighter." %}

Note that high frequency does not always mean damage. While stains are the strongest region, they are not the only non-zero values. The other residuals in the figure can be attributed to differences in misalignment, color, and grain artifacts.

As for the stains, they each appear in one plate only, so no shift can remove it. To remove these defects, we would have to postprocess by predicting the masked pixels and filling in / inpainting the "true" pixels in the aligned rgb image.

### Discussion on Colored Edge Bands
The colored band artifacts along the borders of almost all images results from applying the circular `np.roll` operation, which pushes pixels from one edge to the opposite one.

The width of each band equals to the magnitude of the corresponding shift.

The color of the band depends on which of the channels is wrapped there:
- Where both red and green are wrapped, the band is blue/purple if the wrapped content is darker than the true content, and yellow if it is brighter.
- Where only red is wrapped (e.g., `01269v.jpg` where the red channel is vertically shifted 8 px more than the green channel), the band is cyan if the wrapped content is darker and red if it is brighter.
- When only green is wrapped (e.g., `01725u.jpg`), the band is green. 

These "displaced" pixels do not exist in all three plates, so no shift can align them.

### Discussion on Failure Case `01725u.jpg`
Most importantly, **why does the gaussian pyramid fail on image `01725u.jpg`?**

{% include figure-row.html
   title="01725u.jpg"
   srcs="/assets/a1/01725u/01725u_pyramid_euclidean.jpg, /assets/a1/01725u/01725u_pyramid_ncc.jpg"
   labels="Euclidean, NCC" %}

Visually inspecting the misaligned image, we can see that the algorithm attempted somewhat successfully to correct the misalignment between the green and reference blue channel. However, it failed to find the needed shift to align the red and blue channel. As a result, we see the unshifted red channel appear as a ghost artifact. In fact, we obtained the red offset `[0, 77] px` from both metrics - with zero shift in the y direction.

Let's dive deeper into what the calculated metric looks like at every pixel of the image and at every level of the pyramid.

{% include figure.html src="/assets/a1/level_maps_01725u_ncc.png" caption="NCC score maps for 01725u at every pyramid level, from L7 (coarsest, 41×47) to L1 (full resolution, 2585×2985).<br>Top row is green channel and bottom row is red channel, both aligned to blue. <br>Each map covers ±15 px around the shift the level starts from; brighter is a better match.<br>The red dashed box is the region actually searched (±15 px at L7, ±2 px at finer levels).<br>○ is the shift chosen, and ★ is the reference shift from the 10%-margin pyramid, scaled to that level. We are considering this reference shift as out 'ground truth'." %}

Compare the rightmost plots on both rows (L7 or level 7). In the green channel, we see bright lines corresponding to NCC scores along both horizontal and vertical shifts. In contrast, in the red channel, we only have a bright line along the horizontal shift. This indicates that in the red channel, every downward (or upward) shift comes with a penalty. Zero shift has none - so the best vertical shift just stays at 0.

{% include figure-row.html
   srcs="/assets/a1/01725u/01725u_b.jpg, /assets/a1/01725u/01725u_g.jpg, /assets/a1/01725u/01725u_r.jpg"
   labels="Blue, Green, Red" caption="Preprocessed 01725u.jpg via naive cropping" %}

As we previously discussed, when `np.roll` shifts the plates down, it wraps the rows that fall off the bottom around to the top. The bottom band of the red channel that gets pasted to the top has a much brighter intensity compared to both the roof of the blue channel. This causes a big penalty in the NCC score of the red vertical shift. While both channels, have bands from the bottom wrap to the top, why is red affected so much worse than green?

Firstly, compare the difference in brightness between the bottom panel of the green channel and the red channel. The red channel resembles the blue less than the green channel to begin with.

Secondly, notice how much more misaligned the red image is in the y-direction compared to the green image. The red channel roughly needs twice the shift, so it's pasted strip is twice as large as green's. The reference shifts (plotted as the blue stars) calculated by the 10% margin NCC pyramid, which was able to align this image nicely, are `[2.5 1.2]` for the red channel and `[1.2 0.7]` in the green channel - roughly twice!

In summary, a bigger shift means a brighter, thicker strip in the red channel - leading to a bigger penalty.

Nevertheless, you might also notice that the pyramid chose `[0 0]` shift in L7 for the green channel. Why did the green channel recover?

In L7, green makes a mistake of ~1 px shifts in both directions. In the next higher level, this ~1 px shift error corresponds to ~2 px (`D_est = 2*D_down`). Recall that only the coarsest level had a search window of $\pm 15$ px; the remaining levels only fine-tune the coarse answer by a search window of $\pm 2$ px. Lucky for us, the 2px error falls within this fine search window, and is quickly corrected by the L6 level.

We see the same trend in error in the red channel, where the error between reference and chosen shift doubles as we move from L7 $\to$ L1. Our L7 y-error of ~2.5 px corresponds to 5 px, which already falls outside the $\pm 2$ px search window of subsequent layers. So while our gap doubles, each finer level only permits a correction of 2 px. Moreover, the same pasted-strip penalty keeps favouring zero anyways.

The figure below summarizes the paragraph above nicely. 

{% include figure.html src="/assets/a1/level_dist_01725u_ncc.png" caption="Maximum distance between each level's estimate and reference shift from the 10%-margin pyramid (ground truth shift)." %}

___

The simplest solution for this issue is to just remove that bright, thick band at the bottom of the red channel that is dominating our error. The 10%-margin pyramid crops out 10% of the image margins, excluding the outer rows/columns from the metric score. The pasted strip is never counted and the penalty disappears, allowing our algorithm to select a non-zero shift based on the more central features of our images in level L7.

|------------------------|--------------|--------------|
| Euclidean (10% margin) | [258, -298]  | [77, 45]     |
| NCC (10% margin)       | [162, 78]    | [77, 45]     |
|------------------------|--------------|--------------|
| Euclidean (no margin)  |   [0, 77]    | [77, 44]     |
| NCC (no margin)        |   [0, 77]    | [77, 44]     |

Interestingly, the Euclidean pyramid still fails on `01725u`. While we removed errors resulting from the differences on the margins, Euclidean distance penalizes brightness and contrast differences between plates in addition to structural mismatch. Overall, the red plate is still much brighter and higher-contrast than the blue. On the other hand, the NCC is unaffected because it normalises out mean and contrast.

Generally, the 10% margin performs well on all the images in our dataset - except for its failure case `01269.jpg`. Outputs for the 10% margin pyramids can be found in `naive_crop_results/` with format `{image_id}_pyramid_{euclidean/ncc}_wrap.jpg`.

{% include figure-row.html
   title="01269v.jpg aligned with the 10%-margin Gaussian pyramid"
   srcs="/assets/a1/01269v/01269v_pyramid_euclidean_wrap.jpg, /assets/a1/01269v/01269v_pyramid_ncc_wrap.jpg"
   labels="Euclidean, NCC" %}

While the 10%-margin pyramid was able to correctly align the original failure case by removing a erroneous penalty, the margin here removes border content that was actually informative and supported the correct shift. 

Therefore, the margin technique is not guaranteed to generalize across shift spaces.

A more robust solution would be to score with using edges / gradients, which are features shared across all three plates even when their brightness or contrast differs.
